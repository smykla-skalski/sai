# Usage rules

William Strunk Jr.'s rules of usage and his list of words and expressions commonly misused ("The Elements of Style", 1918), checked against current guides. Fix a usage error only when the fix keeps the meaning, every protected detail and the author's register.

When rules disagree, the higher one wins:

1. The text's own consistent convention, and any house style or style guide the user names.
2. Current plain-language and developer style guides: Google, Microsoft, Digital.gov.
3. Strunk.

An entry says so where current guides have moved on from 1918. Never change text inside quotation marks, code, commands, identifiers or defined terms.

## Punctuation and grammar

### 1. Possessive singular

Add 's to a singular noun whatever its last letter: Charles's friend, Burns's poems, the witch's malice. The exceptions are ancient names in -es and -is (Achilles' heel, often better "the heel of Achilles"), Jesus', and phrases such as "for conscience' sake". Possessive pronouns take no apostrophe: hers, its, ours, theirs, yours. "It's" means "it is".

### 2. Serial comma

In a series of three or more terms with one conjunction, put a comma after each term except the last: "red, white, and blue", "He opened the letter, read it, and made a note of its contents." Google and Microsoft use it too. Keep the text's own choice when it omits the comma throughout. Company names keep their own form: "Brown, Shipley & Co."

### 3. Parenthetic expressions

Enclose a parenthetic expression between two commas, or use none. Never use one comma alone:

| Wrong | Right |
| :-- | :-- |
| Marjorie's husband, Colonel Nelson paid us a visit yesterday. | Marjorie's husband, Colonel Nelson, paid us a visit yesterday. |
| My brother you will be pleased to hear, is now in perfect health. | My brother, you will be pleased to hear, is now in perfect health. |

When a conjunction comes before the parenthetic part, put the first comma before the conjunction: "He saw us coming, and unaware that we had learned of his treachery, greeted us with a smile."

Set off a non-restrictive clause, which adds a fact about something already identified: "The audience, which had at first been indifferent, became more and more interested." Do not set off a restrictive clause, which says which one is meant: "The candidate who best meets these requirements will get the place." Google style uses "which" with commas for the first kind and "that" without commas for the second.

Also set off an introductory phrase or dependent clause before the main clause, "etc." and "Jr.", and the year in a US date inside a sentence ("On April 6, 1917, Congress voted"). Keep the text's own date format.

### 4. Comma before a conjunction that joins clauses

Put a comma before "and", "but", "or", "nor", "for", "so" or "yet" when it joins two independent clauses: "The early records of the city have disappeared, and the story of its first years can no longer be reconstructed." With one subject written once, keep the comma before "but" ("I have heard his arguments, but am still unconvinced") and drop it before "and" when the link is close ("He has several years' experience and is thoroughly competent").

"And" says only that two clauses are related. When the relation is cause or contrast, say so: "Because the early records have disappeared, the story of the city's first years is lost." "Although the situation is perilous, there is still one chance of escape." Replace a clause-opening "so" the same way: "I had never been there before, so I got lost" becomes "Because I had never been there before, I got lost."

### 5. Comma splices

Do not join two independent clauses with a comma alone. Use a period, a semicolon, or a comma with a conjunction. Prefer the period: Microsoft style splits a semicolon sentence into two sentences or a list.

> **Before:** It is nearly half past five, we cannot reach town before dark.
>
> **After:** It is nearly half past five. We cannot reach town before dark.

An adverb such as "however", "therefore", "then", "thus", "accordingly" or "besides" does not join clauses: put a period or a semicolon before it. Short clauses alike in form may take a comma: "Man proposes, God disposes." In chat and other familiar registers, a colloquial splice such as "I hardly knew him, he was so changed" can stay.

### 6. Fragments

Do not split one sentence in two with a period:

| Wrong | Right |
| :-- | :-- |
| I met them on a Cunard liner several years ago. Coming home from Liverpool to New York. | I met them on a Cunard liner several years ago, coming home from Liverpool to New York. |
| He was an interesting talker. A man who had traveled all over the world. | He was an interesting talker, a man who had traveled all over the world. |

A deliberate fragment is fine when the emphasis is warranted ("Again and again he called out. No reply."), and so is a short answer in chat ("Yes. CI is green.").

### 7. Dangling modifiers

A participial phrase, adjective or noun phrase at the start of a sentence must refer to the subject:

| Wrong | Right |
| :-- | :-- |
| On arriving in Chicago, his friends met him at the station. | When he arrived in Chicago, his friends met him at the station. |
| Young and inexperienced, the task seemed easy to me. | Young and inexperienced, I thought the task easy. |
| Without a friend to counsel him, the temptation proved irresistible. | Without a friend to counsel him, he found the temptation irresistible. |
| After running the migration, the table is locked. | After you run the migration, the table is locked. |
| Having finished the review, the migration was approved by the team. | The team finished the review and approved the migration. |

Such sentences are often absurd: "Wondering irresolutely what to do next, the clock struck twelve." The fix keeps the actor the phrase implied: the team that finished the review stays in the sentence.

## Words and expressions

Many of these are "good in their place, but constantly obtruding themselves into places where they have no right to be". When a fix by word swap reads badly, recast the sentence.

| Entry | Rule | Before → after |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| all right | Two words, never "alright". In formal text, say what you mean | "alright" → "all right" |
| as good or better than | Rearrange | "as good or better than his" → "as good as his, or better" |
| as to whether | "Whether" is enough | "as to whether it ships" → "whether it ships" |
| bid | Takes the infinitive without "to". Plainer: "told", "asked" | "bid them to leave" → "told them to leave" |
| but | Not after "doubt" or "help". Do not stack one "but" on another | "no doubt but that" → "no doubt that" |
| can | Ability or an option the reader has. Use "may" for permission and "might" for possibility, and never swap modals in rules or requirements | Keep "You can set a timeout" |
| case | Usually needless, except in terms such as "test case" | "In many cases, the rooms were poorly ventilated" → "Many of the rooms were poorly ventilated" |
| certainly | An intensifier like "very". Cut it | "This certainly helps" → "This helps" |
| character | Often needless | "acts of a hostile character" → "hostile acts" |
| claim (verb) | Means to assert a right or a disputed point. Keep it where the source signals doubt; otherwise "say" | "He claimed he had sent it" → "He said he had sent it" |
| clever | Overused. Keep it for small ingenuity; cut it as vague praise | "a clever platform" → "a platform" |
| compare to, compare with | "To" for likeness between unlike things, "with" for differences between like things | "Compare v2 to v1" → "Compare v2 with v1" |
| consider | No "as" when it means "believe to be" | "I consider him as competent" → "I consider him competent" |
| data | Strunk treats it as plural. Google treats it as a mass noun. Keep the text's consistent choice, else singular | "the data is", "less data" |
| dependable | Strunk preferred "reliable". Both are standard now; leave it | No change |
| different than | Prefer "different from", "other than" or "unlike" before a noun | "different than v1" → "different from v1" |
| divided into | Not for "composed of" | "The poem is divided into stanzas" → "The poem is composed of stanzas" |
| don't, doesn't | "Don't" is "do not". "Does not" is "doesn't" | "It don't work" → "It doesn't work" |
| due to | Means "caused by", after a noun or a form of "be". Not for "because of" | "It failed, due to a timeout" → "It failed because of a timeout" |
| effect, affect | "Effect" (noun) is a result, (verb) to bring about. "Affect" is to influence. Avoid vague "effects" | "This effects latency" → "This affects latency" |
| etc. | Only when the rest is obvious. Never after "such as" or "for example" | "such as Go, Rust, etc." → "such as Go and Rust" |
| fact | Only for what can be verified, not for judgment. Revise "the fact that" out of the sentence | "the fact that it failed" → "its failure" |
| factor | Hackneyed. Say how | "His superior training was the great factor in his winning" → "He won by being better trained" |
| feature | Adds nothing as a filler noun. As a verb, use "has" or "is" | "A feature of the release is a new CLI" → "The release has a new CLI" |
| fix | Strunk wanted it only for "fasten". "Fix a bug" is standard now; leave it | No change |
| folk | Collective noun. In formal text, "people" | "folks" → "people" (formal text only) |
| get | "Have got" means "have". US "gotten" is standard | "I have got a key" → "I have a key" |
| he is a man who | Redundant | "He is a man who is very ambitious" → "He is very ambitious" |
| help | Not "help but" | "could not help but notice" → "could not help noticing" |
| however | Strunk kept "however" (nevertheless) out of first place. Starting with "However," is accepted today. Cut it when nothing contrasts | "At last, however, we reached camp" |
| interesting | Do not announce interest; show it | "An interesting story is told of" → tell the story |
| kind of, sort of | Only in the literal sense ("a kind of resin"). As a hedge, use an equal hedge such as "somewhat" | "kind of slow" → "somewhat slow" |
| less, fewer | "Less" for quantity, "fewer" for number. A round number as an amount takes "less" | "less tables" → "fewer tables"; "less than 100 signers" stays |
| like, as | "Like" before nouns and pronouns, "as" before clauses. "Such as" introduces examples | "He thought like I did" → "He thought as I did" |
| line, along these lines | Overworked | "spoke along the same lines" → "spoke to the same effect" |
| literally | Not for exaggeration | "literally dead with fatigue" → "dead tired" |
| lose out | "Out" or "up" adds nothing here. Keep phrasal verbs whose meaning differs: find out, run out, sign up, set up | "lose out" → "lose"; "win out" → "win" |
| most, almost | Not "most" for "almost" | "most everybody" → "almost everybody" |
| nature | Often needless, often vague | "acts of a hostile nature" → "hostile acts" |
| near by, nearby | Strunk wrote "near" or "neighboring". "Nearby" is standard now; leave it | No change |
| oftentimes, ofttimes | Archaic | "oftentimes" → "often" |
| one hundred and one | Strunk keeps the "and". In technical text, write numerals | "101" |
| one of the most | A threadbare opener. When kept, the verb after "that" or "who" is plural | "one of the ablest engineers that has worked on it" → "that have worked on it" |
| participle for verbal noun | In formal text, a possessive before a verbal noun. Recast when it is clumsy | "the prospect of the Senate accepting" → "the prospect of the Senate's accepting" |
| people, public | "The people" is political. Use "the public" for an audience or customers | "popular with the people" → "popular with the public" |
| phase | A stage of change. Not for "aspect" or "topic" | "another phase of the subject" → "another point" |
| possess | Not for "have" or "own" | "He possessed great courage" → "He had great courage" |
| prove | Strunk wanted "proved". "Proven" is standard now, mostly as an adjective | No change |
| respective, respectively | Usually omit | "listed under their respective authors" → "listed under their authors" |
| shall, will | Strunk's first-person rule is obsolete. For an obligation, "must" is plainer (Digital.gov). Google avoids "shall" except on legal advice | "The engineer shall notify" → "The engineer must notify", listed as a changed detail |
| should | A protected modal. Keep it; never turn it into "must" or drop it | No change |
| so | Not as an intensifier in formal text. For a clause, see rule 4 | "so fast" → "fast" or "very fast" |
| split infinitive | Strunk avoided it; current guides allow it. Move the adverb only when the sentence reads as well and means the same | "to diligently inquire" → "to inquire diligently"; "to more than double" stays |
| state | Means to express fully. Not for "say" | "He stated that he would come" → "He said he would come" |
| student body | "Students" | "a member of the student body" → "a student" |
| system | Often needless | "the dormitory system" → "dormitories" |
| thanking you in advance | Thank after the help, or ask politely | "Thanking you in advance" → "Thanks" |
| they | Strunk wanted "he" after "each" or "anybody". Google and Microsoft use singular "they". Keep it; never change it to "he", "he or she" or "s/he" | "Each user enters their password" stays |
| very | Use sparingly. Cut it when the degree does not matter; otherwise use one word of the same strength | "very small" → "tiny" |
| viewpoint | Not for "view" or "opinion" | "in my viewpoint" → "in my view" |
| while | Only for time ("during the time that"), per Strunk and Microsoft. Not for "and", "but", "although" or "whereas" | "The office is downstairs, while the rest is the factory" → "The office is downstairs. The rest of the building is the factory." |
| whom | Not before "he said" when it is the subject of the next verb | "the man whom he thought was his friend" → "the man who he thought was his friend" |
| worth while | Now one word, "worthwhile". Vague approval; say what it is worth | "His books are not worthwhile" → "His books are not worth reading" |
| would | For a habit, use the simple past. A conditional "would" is a protected modal; keep it | "Once a year he would visit" → "Once a year he visited" |

## From current guides

| Entry | Rule | Before → after |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| since | Use "because" for cause. "Since" can also mean time (Google) | "since it failed" → "because it failed" |
| via | Google says do not use it. Say "through", "by" or "with" | "via the CLI" → "with the CLI" |
| easy, simple, simply | What is easy for you might not be easy for others (Google). Cut them | "Simply run `make`" → "Run `make`" |
| please | In instructions, cut it. In a request to a person, keep the politeness | "Please click Save" → "Click Save"; "Could you please review it?" stays |
